### D. Coordination cost exceeds task cost — measured numbers

- FIRST-PARTY Anthropic (own production system): "In our data, agents typically use about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats. On BrowseComp, token usage ALONE explains 80% of the performance variance (with tool calls and model choice the other two factors). Multi-agent (Opus 4 lead + Sonnet 4 subagents) beat single-agent Opus 4 by **90.2%** on their internal research eval. - Same first-party source, on domains where coordination does NOT pay: "some domains that require all agents to share the same context or involve many dependencies between agents are not a good fit for multi-agent systems today. Fetch date 2026-10-20. Confidence: primary. [Plugin implication: first-party evidence says the shared-context / heavy-dependency case is where multi-agent is weakest — precisely the case a shared room targets. - **Deadlock under resource contention is protocol-determined, not capability-determined.** Hasan & BusiReddyGari, "DPBench: Structural Determinants of Multi-Agent LLM Coordination Under Simultaneous Resource Contention", arXiv 2602.13255 (2026-02-02). Single-round messaging and memory of past timesteps do NOT change the rate at the sample size run. Confidence: primary. [Plugin implication: THE key finding for our three blocking waits. Our waits (wait_for_claim, wait_for_agent, wait_for_message) are exactly a wait-for-graph. ## Conflicting evidence
- **Does coordination pay at all?** MAST (arXiv 2503.13657) opens with "performance gains on popular benchmarks are often minimal" and reports 75% failure for ChatDev on ProgramDev; 2512.08296 v3 reports overall mean MAS improvement of **−3.5%** (CI [−18.6%, +25.7%]) across benchmarks. BUT Anthropic's first-party production eval reports multi-agent beating single-agent by **+90.2%** on their internal research eval, and Gao et al. (2505.18286) find MAS>MAS on AIME (the hardest dataset). Reconciliation from the primary sources themselves: benefit is task- and architecture-contingent (decomposable/parallelizable → gains; sequential/shared-context → losses). Do not report either side alone.
- **Collaborative scaling law exists or not.** Qian et al. 2406.07155 (ICLR 2025) report a logistic collaborative scaling law over >1000 agents with irregular topologies > regular. The newer 2512.08296 v3 cites Wang et al. as noting "collaborative scaling shows no significant universal pattern" and reports a **negative** net mean. Both are primary; the newer paper has a controlled design (matched tokens/tools/prompts) that the older one lacks.
- **Overhead coefficient significance flips across scaling-paper versions.** 2512.08296 **v1** (4 benchmarks, N=180): O%×T β = −0.141, p<0.001 (significant), R²_CV = 0.513. **v3** (6 benchmarks, N=260): O%×T β = −0.033, **p = 0.211 (NOT significant)**, R²_CV = 0.373 (0.413 with task-grounded capability metric); Ec×T shrank from −0.330 (p<0.001) to −0.096 (p=0.002); PS_A×log(1+n_a) from −0.408 to −0.236. The v1 text also says "all systems matched for total reasoning tokens (mean μ=4,800 per trial)" — i.e. token budgets were MATCHED, so "overhead 58/263/285/515%" is overhead in TURNS/communication at matched token budget, not extra spend. Any citation of this paper must state which version. **Cite v3 for the abstract-level numbers (+80.8%/−70.0%, 260 configs, 6 benchmarks, 45% threshold, 87% accuracy); treat the "overhead scales super-linearly with complexity" coefficient as version-fragile** (direction preserved, significance lost).
- **Error amplification metric is double-edged.** Task-level A_e ≈ 1.1–1.3 (coordination roughly neutral on final error rate) vs trace-level A_e = 4.4–17.2× (coordination massively inflates wasted work). Same paper, both primary. Pick the metric that matches the claim.
- **Redundancy helps vs hurts.** 2512.08296 v3: R×n_a β = +0.024 (p = 0.034) — redundancy is a *benefit* — yet the same paper reports high redundancy (R > 0.50) correlating NEGATIVELY with success (r = −0.136, p = 0.004). Both in the primary text. Reading: redundancy is worth ~5% and cannot pay for overhead; beyond a threshold it hurts.
- **Single-agent beats multi-agent on the same benchmark.** Gao et al. show ~80% ties on datapoints plus real SAS-wins. HiddenBench shows MAS 30.1% vs single-agent-with-full-info 80.7% — but the comparison is NOT apples-to-apples (the single agent gets the complete information; the MAS must pool it). The gap is attributed to a mechanism (latent information asymmetry), not to the MAS having more information.
- **Multi-agent debate: helpful or harmful.** Du et al. (2305.14325) and the debate literature report gains; Wynn et al. (2509.05396) report harm, with a measured mechanism (correct→incorrect flips dominate) and a measured non-fix (correctness-payoff prompts do not help). Same protocol family, different results — the deciding variables they identify are task type, agent capability mix, and social conditioning.
- **Anthropic's own production system is simultaneously the best multi-agent result and the best "don't" result.** +90.2% over single-agent Opus 4 on research, AND "most coding tasks… are not a good fit for multi-agent systems today". Both first-party, same page.
- **Cost comparison denominator.** AgentPrune's "$5.6 vs $43.7" is topology-vs-topology, NOT multi-agent-vs-single-agent. Gao et al.'s 4–220× is the actual MAS-vs-SAS ratio. Keep them separate; do not combine into one multiplier.
- **MAST's own numbers differ between arXiv v2 and the NeurIPS camera version** (v2: "over 200 execution traces across 7 task domains"; camera: "1600+ annotated traces across 7 popular MAS frameworks"; v2 abstract: "200 conversation traces"). Same paper, later revision. Use the camera version's numbers when citing corpus size.
